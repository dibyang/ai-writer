# 通用小说审稿规则

Apply this file to every fiction review before any genre-specific module.

## Baseline Questions

A publishable chapter must let an ordinary reader answer:

- What happened in this chapter?
- Who wants what?
- Who is under pressure or danger?
- What changed by the end?
- Why continue to the next chapter?

If the answer is only atmosphere, explanation, lore, or mood, the chapter fails fiction momentum.

## Core Dimensions

Review these for all novels:

1. Story movement: each chapter must add a new event, choice, conflict, discovery, danger, reversal, or emotional consequence.
2. Character agency: major characters must act from desire, fear, duty, guilt, love, ambition, survival, or concealment, not only to deliver information.
3. Motivation clarity: readers should understand why the protagonist cannot simply walk away.
4. Scene continuity: scene changes must orient the reader through place, time, body, objective, and pressure.
5. Narrative prose: this is a novel, not a screenplay. Do not judge only dialogue and actions; check narration, internal pressure, sensory continuity, and cognitive movement.
6. Reader readability: professional or historical detail is allowed, but the immediate stakes must be plain.
7. Emotional progression: trace what triggered the feeling, what it means to this person, and how it affects attention, restraint, speech, choice, or later behavior. Use the beats the scene needs; do not impose one emotional sequence on every character.
8. Information economy: avoid repeated explanations, inert exposition, or scenes that can be deleted without changing the story.
9. Language texture: prose should be clear, concrete, and character-bound. Avoid abstract summary where scene pressure is needed.
10. Ordinary-word standard: avoid half-classical, over-condensed, or jargon-heavy wording when a normal reader must understand consequences quickly.

## All-Character Humanity Rule

The human-authenticity rule applies to every meaningful character, not only the protagonist. A novel fails character review if only the viewpoint character feels alive while supporting characters, victims, antagonists, servants, witnesses, officials, family members, lovers, mentors, or informants exist only to deliver clues, create pressure, explain rules, or trigger the protagonist's growth.

For each meaningful character in a scene, review whether they have at least one visible human layer:

- desire: what they want right now, even if small or selfish;
- fear: what they are trying not to lose;
- constraint: status, money, family, law, body, reputation, hunger, illness, duty, debt, love, or shame;
- coping style: silence, bargaining, obedience, control, cruelty, humor, denial, calculation, confession, flight, or protection;
- contradiction: what they say versus what their body, timing, or choices reveal;
- private cost: what this scene takes from them, even if the plot does not pause to explain it.

Minor characters do not all need long backstory, but they must not feel like props. A gatekeeper should fear punishment or want a bribe; a servant should know who can starve her family; an antagonist should protect an interest or self-image; a victim should have tried something before becoming evidence; a colleague should have their own procedure, pressure, and doubt.

Hard blocker: if several characters can be described only by plot function, such as clue giver, rescuer, obstacle, henchman, crying family member, villain executor, or information window, the chapter is below publication line until at least the important ones gain human motive, pressure, and behavioral specificity.

## Character Baseline and Reaction Consistency Rule

Major characters must have a reviewable behavioral baseline before the review judges whether a reaction is convincing. A baseline is not a list of adjectives; it is the repeated pattern of what this person wants, fears, notices, avoids, says, hides, and does under pressure.

For each major character, check whether the manuscript has established or implied:

- core desire, fear, wound, duty, guilt, ambition, or survival need;
- profession, training, social status, historical constraint, and power position;
- default self-protection style: attack, retreat, silence, humor, control, obedience, concealment, bargaining, evidence-gathering, or sacrifice;
- speech rhythm and information habit: direct, evasive, formal, sarcastic, careful, impulsive, withholding, or over-explaining;
- pressure threshold: what makes this person lose control, freeze, lie, take risks, or betray a stated principle;
- relationship triggers: who can make this person soften, panic, disobey, become cruel, or act irrationally.

Every important reaction must arise from that baseline. Pressure may bend or break the baseline, but the scene must show the trigger, the break point, and the consequence. If a character acts against established traits only because the plot needs it, mark it as a character-logic failure.

Hard-review questions:

1. Would this specific person notice this first, or is it the author/AI choosing the most convenient detail?
2. Would this specific person speak this way under their status, profession, fear, and relationship pressure?
3. Does the action match their established coping pattern, or does it switch to generic cleverness/calmness/bravery?
4. If the reaction contradicts the baseline, has the text shown a strong enough trigger and aftershock?
5. Do different characters respond differently, or do they all sound like the same efficient reasoning voice?

A publishable novel should make readers feel that important reactions are shaped by these characters' histories and habits, even when two people happen to make the same outward choice.

For a shared event involving several important characters, or a meaningful change across chapters, use `character-emotion-consistency.md` to compare what each person knows and stands to lose, what they first notice or protect, what they say or withhold, and what later behavior changes. Similar outward reactions may have different causes; one person's atypical reaction may be earned by new pressure or experience. Do not infer a contradiction from a personality label alone.

## Character-Bound Polishing Rule

When giving polishing suggestions or applying polishing after explicit user authorization, do not only make sentences smoother. First preserve and strengthen the character's behavioral baseline.

Polishing should adjust:

- perception order: what this character would notice first;
- reaction order: select the perception, bodily, habitual, verbal, and action beats this person and this moment need, rather than imposing one sequence on every character;
- diction: words that match the character's education, status, profession, era, and emotional control;
- silence and omission: what this character would refuse to say or be unable to say;
- action choice: what this character would actually do under their fear, desire, duty, or relationship pressure;
- residue: how the moment changes the next line, next scene, or next decision.

Do not polish a character into a generic clever narrator. If the original sentence is readable but characterless, the polishing task is to make it more character-bound, not merely more elegant.

## Chapter Checklist

Ask these in every chapter review:

1. Does this chapter add new information?
2. Does it create a new question?
3. Does it move the main line?
4. Does it sharpen character conflict?
5. Does it end with a reason to keep reading?
6. Is any dialogue only delivering known facts?
7. Is any description repeated without new pressure?
8. Is there a logic gap?
9. Is there a recoverable clue, emotional debt, or relationship shift?
10. If deleted, would the story still stand?

If deletion barely affects the story, the chapter needs cutting or rewriting.

## Review Output

For a single chapter, give:

- verdict and score if requested;
- hard blockers first;
- ordinary-reader readability;
- character/scene/prose notes;
- small polishing suggestions only after structural issues.

For a batch, give:

- overall verdict;
- chapter table;
- cross-chapter risks;
- publication blockers if any;
- next-revision priorities.
