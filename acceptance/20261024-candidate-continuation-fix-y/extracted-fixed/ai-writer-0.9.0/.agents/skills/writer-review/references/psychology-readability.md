# 心理描写与普通读者可读性硬规则

Apply `general-review.md` first. Use this file when the user asks about readability, psychological detail, ordinary-reader understanding, professionality versus clarity, or when major identity/danger/emotional moments appear.

## Ordinary Reader Rule

Professional writing does not mean obscure writing. Let expert readers feel accuracy and ordinary readers grasp danger.

Across a high-stakes sequence, readers should be able to follow the character's response. Check the following where relevant; no single paragraph must carry them all:

- what triggered the reaction;
- what the body or behavior gives away, if anything;
- what old knowledge, habit, or memory is pulled in, if relevant;
- what current danger invalidates easy explanations;
- what choice the character makes next.

## Major Psychological Scene Standard

Major psychological nodes cannot be solved by one or two explanatory sentences. They need to read like a scene, not a patch.

For nodes carrying opening retention, identity shock, institutional danger, betrayal, evidence reversal, or a chapter hook, use the following beats as diagnostic lenses. Select and order those the character and scene need; do not fill all eight into every scene or give different characters the same sequence:

1. External trigger: sound, object, touch, name, phrase, wound, document, or action hits first.
2. Body or behavioral reaction: a hand, breath, sightline, step, routine, or deliberate stillness may register pressure before explanation.
3. Old-world return: memory, job habit, prior case, family, training, or earlier scene flashes back.
4. Reality pressure: concrete present details prove this cannot be hand-waved.
5. Cognitive testing: the character tries one explanation, then it fails.
6. Keyword pressure: a name, legal term, object, title, or phrase repeats and gains weight.
7. Delayed emotion: fear, shame, shock, grief, or anger arrives after confusion or professional control.
8. Action choice: silence, retreat, question, refuse, remember, record, hide, follow, or confront.

If readers only know “she was shocked/alert/sad” but cannot feel how this particular person got there, the scene is thin. A restrained scene may carry that process through an omission, a changed question, a failed routine, or one costly choice rather than a long internal monologue.

## Abnormal Entry Rule

For first dream-entry, transmigration, time-slip, or impossible awakening, include at least:

- lost modern anchors: phone, ID, notebook, familiar clothing, lights, workplace;
- sensory reality: cold, wet, smell, pressure, pain, sound, texture;
- world mismatch: unfamiliar language, architecture, clothing, tool, rule, title;
- knowledge trigger: professional or lived knowledge connects the danger;
- no safe test: she cannot risk treating it as a dream;
- survival switch: she chooses the least dangerous action.

## Concrete Consequence Rule

Institutional, legal, historical, or professional danger must be written as a concrete consequence.

Prefer clear outcomes:

- be taken away for questioning;
- be written into a charge;
- be beaten, detained, isolated, silenced, misidentified, or removed from records;
- lose access, credibility, identity, custody, evidence, or witness protection.

Avoid condensed phrases that require translation when stakes should be instant.

## Novel, Not Screenplay

Do not reduce review to camera movement, dialogue, and blocking. A novel scene also needs:

- internal pressure;
- narrative selection;
- cognition under stress;
- sensory continuity;
- sentence rhythm matching mental state;
- consequences that outlast the visible action.

## Professional Clarity Rule

Making prose easier for ordinary readers should not lower professionality. Keep professionality in evidence, process, procedure, and constraint; make danger, emotion, and choice immediately understandable.

## Authentic Human Reaction Rule

Extreme scenes must simulate real human psychology, not only a plot-useful emotional summary. A character under shock, danger, grief, betrayal, institutional pressure, or impossible evidence should not react like a clean reasoning engine.

Check whether the reaction includes human friction:

- involuntary body response before clean thought: breath, hands, throat, stomach, vision, skin, balance, temperature, pain;
- attention narrowing or misdirection: the character notices one concrete, sometimes irrational detail before understanding the whole event;
- failed normal habit: reaching for a phone, badge, pocket, tool, familiar procedure, familiar person, or old routine that no longer works;
- delayed comprehension: the character does not instantly understand the full meaning; the realization arrives in steps;
- self-protection or avoidance: denial, bargaining, silence, checking small facts, not wanting to look, not wanting to say a name, or choosing a smaller immediate goal;
- contradiction: professional control and human fear can coexist; love, suspicion, shame, anger, and duty may pull against each other;
- consequence after the moment: the reaction changes later speech, trust, body movement, memory, or decision.

If the text jumps directly from event to correct analysis, thematic statement, or efficient decision, check for AI-like psychological writing. Mark it only when the character lacks an established reason for that response and the scene gives no meaningful leak, friction, or later consequence; a practiced professional can act quickly without becoming emotionless.

## Anti-AI Psychology Checklist

Flag a scene when any of these appear in a major psychological node:

- The character names the emotion but the reader cannot feel how it formed.
- The character processes danger in a perfectly linear sequence: observe, infer, decide, with no bodily lag or emotional interference.
- The reaction mainly explains plot stakes to the reader instead of showing what the character can bear, avoid, misunderstand, or choose.
- Every character has the same calm, compressed, analytical response style.
- The scene uses elegant abstract lines where a human would first have messy sensory, bodily, or practical reactions.
- The character instantly accepts an impossible event, betrayal, death, or institutional threat without testing, denial, stunned silence, or partial misreading.
- A supposedly devastating moment leaves no residue in later movement, speech, trust, sleep, appetite, attention, or decision.

For publication review, a major node with AI-like psychology is a hard blocker even if the plot logic is sound. Score it below the publication line until the scene is rebuilt as lived human experience.

## Supporting Character Humanity Rule

Psychological authenticity is not a protagonist-only standard. When reviewing or polishing a scene, check the emotional reality of every character who changes the pressure of the scene.

Supporting characters can be concise, but they still need human logic:

- a witness may hide facts because speaking will cost work, marriage, safety, or family;
- a servant may obey cruelty because the household controls food, debt, kin, or shelter;
- an official may follow procedure because a wrong record can destroy their post;
- an antagonist may sound calm because they have rehearsed the moral excuse that lets them live with harm;
- a victim may cooperate, lie, delay, or refuse rescue because someone else will pay if they act freely;
- a colleague may doubt the protagonist because their own professional duty demands doubt.

During polishing, do not merely make these characters more vivid with description. Give them pressure-specific behavior: what they protect, what they avoid saying, what detail they notice first, what they risk by helping, what they gain by harming, and how their body or speech leaks their position.

If a scene's emotional truth depends on a supporting character, that character must not be reduced to a reaction button for the protagonist.

## Character-Specific Psychology Rule

Psychological detail must be character-specific, not generic human fear pasted onto the plot. Before judging a major emotional or danger scene, identify the character's baseline from `general-review.md`: profession, training, status, wound, fear, relationship pressure, self-protection style, and usual speech/attention pattern.

Then check whether the psychological beats are customized:

- a professional may first reach for procedure, evidence, tools, record, diagnosis, chain of custody, or risk control, but the body should still leak fear or shock;
- a powerless or low-status character may first scan authority, punishment, witnesses, exits, speech taboos, and who can be blamed;
- a controlling character may withhold, redirect, ask questions, or manage the room before admitting feeling;
- a protective character may notice who is exposed before noticing their own pain;
- a guilty character may misread neutral details as accusation;
- a suspicious character may treat help as danger until trust is earned;
- an impulsive character may act before understanding, then pay for the mistake.

Do not accept the same reaction style for everyone: calm analysis, neat inner monologue, perfect inference, and clean emotional labels create AI-like sameness. The review should ask what this person would miss, distort, suppress, or over-focus on because of who they already are.

For a consequential departure from an established baseline, check three kinds of evidence:

1. a visible trigger strong enough to crack the old pattern;
2. a bodily, verbal, relational, or tactical cost;
3. residue in later behavior, trust, speech, sleep, attention, or choices.

An arc-turning change usually needs all three across the scene and its aftermath; a smaller departure may be justified by a clear trigger and one visible cost or residue. Do not add inner exposition solely to fill this list. If a pivotal reversal has no support, it reads as author convenience rather than character change.


## Chapter-Level Psychological Gate

Every chapter that contains psychological description, emotional reaction, fear, shock, grief, suspicion, desire, hesitation, guilt, trust, betrayal, institutional pressure, or character choice must be reviewed against the character's established baseline. This is not limited to the protagonist.

Before passing the chapter, identify for each important psychological node:

- whose mind/body is under pressure;
- that character's known personality, profession, status, fear, wound, duty, relationship pressure, and default coping style;
- what this specific character would notice first;
- what ordinary habit, professional reflex, social reflex, or self-protection reflex would fail or take over;
- how the reaction changes the next line, movement, speech, trust, or decision.

A chapter does not pass this gate if psychological writing is merely a small sentence patch such as adding one body reaction, one thought, or one abstract emotional label. For any meaningful psychological node, require a character-specific causal sequence. Major nodes need enough of the diagnostic beats above to make the response felt and the choice understandable; smaller nodes may be concise, using a trigger and a character-specific perception, omission, action, or residue. Do not require the same number or order of beats from every character.

When reviewing a batch, do not say "minor polish" if several chapters contain generic psychology. Mark it as a publication risk or blocker depending on severity. The expected revision direction is not "add more inner monologue"; it is to rebuild perception order, sentence rhythm, bodily behavior, failed habit, and later consequence so the scene feels like this person, not a competent generic narrator.

Hard blocker: if a chapter's important psychological moment could belong to any character after changing the name, or if it reads like one or two explanatory sentences added after the plot was already built, the chapter is below the publication line until that node is rebuilt as character-bound lived experience.
## Psychological Polishing Rule

For polishing, the target is not "add more inner monologue." The target is to rebuild the moment so the reader can feel a specific human mind under pressure.

A psychological polish should usually change several of these, not just add one explanatory sentence:

- external trigger: make the object, sound, name, wound, rule, or gesture hit the character first;
- bodily leak: add or sharpen the involuntary reaction before clean analysis;
- failed habit: let the character reach for a familiar tool, phrase, role, procedure, or relationship that no longer works;
- partial misunderstanding: let comprehension arrive late or wrong before it becomes accurate;
- character-specific focus: choose details this person would over-notice because of profession, fear, guilt, status, love, or suspicion;
- sentence rhythm: shorten, fragment, delay, repeat, or withhold according to the mental state;
- choice under limit: end with a small survivable action instead of a complete explanation;
- aftershock: let the polished moment affect the next exchange, movement, trust, or decision.

A polished paragraph should pass this test: if the name is removed, the reaction still points to this character and not to any competent protagonist.

## Extreme Situation Simulation Standard

For life-threatening, identity-breaking, impossible, grief-heavy, or betrayal scenes, the review should ask:

1. What would this specific person notice first, before they understand the event?
2. What normal habit would fail in this situation?
3. What bodily reaction arrives before language?
4. What wrong or incomplete explanation would they try first?
5. What concrete detail makes denial impossible?
6. What emotion arrives late, after confusion or control?
7. What small action do they choose because they cannot solve the whole crisis yet?
8. How does this moment alter the next scene, not just this paragraph?

A strong revision should not merely add more inner monologue. It should change perception order, sentence rhythm, body behavior, and later choices so the reader feels a human mind trying to survive the moment.
